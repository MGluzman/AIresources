ADA guidelines — what they mean for your course materials

The ADA requires that digital course materials be accessible to students with disabilities. The technical standard used to measure this is WCAG 2.2 AA — but you don't need to memorize the spec. Here's what it means in practice.

1

Perceivable — can everyone access the content?

Text alternatives for images (alt text). Captions for videos. Enough color contrast (4.5:1 for body text). Don't convey meaning with color alone — "the red items are due Friday" leaves colorblind students guessing.

2

Operable — can everyone navigate it?

Everything should work with a keyboard (not just a mouse). No time limits that can't be adjusted. No flashing content that could trigger seizures. Links should say where they go — "click here" tells nobody anything.

3

Understandable — is the language clear?

Use plain language where possible. Define abbreviations and jargon. Make sure forms and instructions are unambiguous. Structure your content with real headings — not just bold text.

4

Robust — does it work with assistive technology?

Use accessible document formats. In Brightspace, use the heading styles, not just visual styling. Export PDFs with proper tags. If you use a table, include header rows.

Two requirements that surprise people

Resize text

Text should be readable when zoomed to 200% without losing content or functionality. Students with low vision routinely zoom their browsers. If your layout breaks at 200%, information gets lost.

WCAG 1.4.4 AA

Headings and labels

Headings should describe the topic or purpose. A long document with no headings is extremely hard to navigate for screen reader users — and for anyone skim-reading, honestly.

Universal Design for Learning

UDL is a framework that asks us to build flexibility into how we teach — not retrofit accommodations after the fact. Think of it as designing a course that works for the widest possible range of learners from the start.

Developed by CAST, UDL has three core principles:

Multiple means of engagement

Why are we learning this? Give students options for how they engage with the material and the course. Connect content to their interests. Offer choice in participation. Build in self-regulation supports.

Addresses anxiety, attention, motivation

Multiple means of representation

How is information presented? Offer content in more than one format — text + audio, visual + verbal. Activate prior knowledge. Build vocabulary explicitly. This isn't dumbing things down; it's widening the door.

Addresses cognitive load, language barriers, sensory differences

Multiple means of action and expression

How do students show what they know? Don't default to one format for all assessments. Some students write beautifully; others think better visually or verbally. Varying assessment types reveals more of what students actually know.

Addresses motor, language, learning differences

"The goal of UDL is not to make things easier — it's to remove unnecessary barriers while keeping appropriate challenges."

The full UDL guidelines and practical classroom strategies live at cast.org. It's genuinely useful browsing.

Did you know?

Small changes to how you prepare course materials can make a real difference for a lot of students — not just those with documented disabilities.

Images without alt text are invisible to screen readers

Did you know that a screen reader skips right over an image with no alt text? For a blind student, that chart or diagram simply doesn't exist. A single sentence of description changes everything.

Bold text isn't the same as a heading

Did you know that screen readers navigate documents by heading structure, not visual formatting? Bold text looks like a heading but isn't one. Using real heading styles in Word or Brightspace takes seconds and makes a huge difference.

Captions help far more students than you'd expect

Did you know that captions are used by students who are deaf or hard of hearing, non-native English speakers, students with auditory processing differences — and anyone studying somewhere they can't play audio? Auto-captions are a decent starting point.

About 1 in 12 men has some form of color blindness

Did you know that if you use color alone to convey meaning — red for late work, green for correct — a significant portion of your students can't reliably see that distinction? Pair color with a label, icon, or pattern.

"Print to PDF" creates an inaccessible document

Did you know that a PDF created by printing to PDF is essentially a flat image? Screen readers can't read it, and it can't be reflowed for small screens. Exporting as a tagged PDF from Word or Google Docs takes one extra click.

Not everyone can use a mouse

Did you know that students with motor disabilities, repetitive strain injuries, or tremors often navigate entirely by keyboard? If your course content or linked tools aren't keyboard-navigable, those students may be locked out entirely.

A quiz isn't the only way to show mastery

Did you know that over-relying on a single format — like selected-response tests or papers — systematically disadvantages students with a range of disabilities and learning differences? Evaluating learning through varied formats gives all students a genuine opportunity to demonstrate what they know.

Multimodal content reaches more learners

Did you know that presenting the same idea in multiple formats — text, diagram, audio, example — isn't redundant, it's inclusive? Students process information differently. A concept that clicks for one student as a written explanation might only land for another through a visual or a worked example. Offering both costs little and gains a lot.
